Rooftop wind farm CFD

A CFD study optimising wind turbine placement on high-rise rooftops in dense urban environments, using Canary Wharf as the case study.

WHENMSc dissertation, 2025
TOOLSANSYS CFX, Design Modeler
SUPERVISORDr Konstantin Volkov

I modelled a realistic 200 m tall, 64 m × 40 m building with site-specific wind data, a reference velocity of 5.858 m/s at 200 m height, to study where rooftop turbines should sit.

Design Modeler geometry of the 200 m high rise

01

Approach

Turbine blades were modelled as a porous medium to simulate swept-volume flow resistance and energy absorption, rather than fully resolving blade geometry, a standard simplification for comparing layout-level performance across many turbines.

I compared two rooftop configurations, hexagonal (staggered) and row-column (grid), across a range of flow resistance coefficients (k = 0 to 1.5), looking at wake recovery, turbulence intensity and total power output for each.

Mesh refinement around the porous turbine cylinders, part of the 2.2 million element mesh

02

Key results

The hexagonal layout outperformed the row-column layout in total power output at every resistance coefficient tested, for example 9,410 W vs 7,121 W at k = 1.5. The staggered arrangement reduced direct wake interference and let velocity recover faster downstream. It came at a cost: only 7 turbines fit in the hexagonal layout on the modelled roof, against 9 in row-column, a real trade-off between aerodynamic efficiency and space utilisation.

03

Takeaway

Turbine layout geometry is a major lever for urban wind energy performance, a finding that matches published literature (Hansen, Hau) but grounded here in a realistic high-rise building model rather than an idealised open field.

Next, I'd want to: resolve full blade geometry instead of the porous-medium simplification, test intermediate k values between 0 and 1.5, and run a second building footprint to see how much of the hexagonal advantage is site-specific.
