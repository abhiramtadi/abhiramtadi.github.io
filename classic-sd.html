Reverse engineering a 58 mm multi-tool

No manufacturer CAD or published drawings exist for this tool, so I measured the real thing and rebuilt every part in SolidWorks. The assembly is finished, with all 16 parts (22 components) modelled and mated, documented in drawings, and printed as a physical prototype.

WHENSelf directed, 2026
TOOLSSolidWorks, digital calipers, Bambu Lab A1
ENVELOPE58 × 18 × 9 mm
01

How I measured it

Functional dimensions came off digital vernier calipers: pivot diameters, hole centres, layer thicknesses. Each one was cross checked against the 58 by 18 by 9 mm overall envelope so small errors could not stack up across the layers.

The free form tool profiles cannot be measured that way, so those were traced from photographs and rebuilt as proper sketch geometry.

02

One datum scheme for everything

Every part uses the same origin, at the rear pivot centre, with the X axis running through both pin centres.

That single decision is what keeps the layer stack up honest and makes the assembly mates predictable instead of something I have to fight each time a part changes.

03

Every part modelled in detail

16 parts and 22 components in total (see the BOM below), each modelled as real geometry rather than an approximation of the outside shape. The main parts are described here. Where there is manufacturing detail, it is in the solid.

Scales removed: liners, brass pivot pins, blade, nail file and scissors

04

Two spring configurations

The back springs are preloaded once they're inside the tool, so one solid body can't describe both states. The as-manufactured (free) shape is the master part, and the seated shape is a driven configuration built from it.

The finished assembly

07

Physical prototype

I printed every part and assembled them into a full-size model to check fit, clearances and how the layers stack. It took three printed iterations, revising the CAD model between prints to correct clearances and tolerances. The blade, nail file and scissors pivot out and fold back in, and the toothpick and tweezers slide into their channels in the scales.

The scissors are three parts in CAD: two blades and the spring. For printing I combined them into one print-in-place body with designed clearance gaps, so they still open and close.

The printed back springs give a light snap at the open and closed positions, but plastic can't store and return energy like spring steel, so they hold far less firmly than the real tool. Being plastic, the tools can't cut or file, and the print leaves out the Swiss cross and tang markings and shows visible layer lines. It's a fit-and-motion model, not a working tool.
